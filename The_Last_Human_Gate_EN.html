01The starting point

Why project reviews could be a starting point for engineers deploying AI

Before a company buys software, connects two systems or launches an application, several specialists need to review the project. Are the contract terms acceptable? Is the data adequately protected? Do the test results support going live? AI does not make those questions disappear. It could, however, change who answers them.

That is the starting point of The Last Human Gate, a study by Jeremy Canale. It asks a specific question: can an AI agent carry out a project review through to the final decision, without a specialist having to redo its work? It then asks a second, quite different question: how much human work would remain? [1]

The title refers to Forward Deployed Engineers, or FDEs. The presentations describe engineers who work directly with a client’s teams to turn an AI demonstration into a system people can use every day. They identify the need, build the solution, connect it to company applications and data, deploy it and keep it running. Their work is not limited to project governance. [1]

The author nevertheless proposes governance reviews as a promising starting point. Similar questions recur across projects; many of the rules are already written down; one review’s conclusions feed the next; and the result can be tested. These features make the work easier to specify and evaluate. [3]

01

Recurring project files

The same review approach can serve multiple projects when the information, rules and scope are comparable.

02

Written decision rules

Thresholds and permissions can become software checks. Less structured situations still need interpretation.

03

Findings that carry forward

A shared record helps prevent findings from getting lost or being copied repeatedly between teams.

04

Results that can be tested

Prepared cases can test whether a review is correct, rather than merely whether its wording is convincing.

An FDE’s own AI deployments also have to pass the company’s reviews. Automating those reviews could make later deployments easier. That would require someone responsible for the whole process, rather than a single department. The hours FDEs spend building and supporting the system must also count towards its cost. The claim that governance will be automated before other business activities is an adoption hypothesis, not an observed sequence. [3]

02Understanding the DGF

What has to be checked before a project can move forward?

The study uses Digital Governance Framework, or DGF, for the required reviews a project must pass. A gate is one checkpoint along that route. The reviewer examines the available documents and records, identifies problems, requests any necessary corrections and makes a decision. The job involves much more than filling in an “approved” box. [2]

Not every project follows the same route. The benchmark distinguishes buying a solution, integrating existing systems and developing an application. The first two routes have six reviews each; the third has five. [2]

Several specialists can read the same project file without doing the same job. The legal reviewer and the security reviewer look for different problems. The final General review considers the earlier conclusions alongside the project’s budget and its fit with company strategy. [2]

These questions summarize the policies supplied in the benchmark. They are not complete job descriptions or a universal process that every company is required to use. [2]

The test evaluates the review, not the agent’s ability to get every project approved. When a proposal should be rejected, a properly justified rejection is the correct outcome. [5]

03The paper’s proposal

What would an agent need to take over a specialist’s review?

The paper separates the required review from the job title of the person who currently performs it. A review can be mandatory without its execution being inherently human. But an AI system has to satisfy three conditions before it can take over. [3]

01

Access to the necessary information

The agent must be able to consult the relevant documents and records, or request missing information where the rules allow it. A claim that backups exist does not establish that a restore test succeeded.

02

A reliable review result

It must handle difficult and incomplete cases, identify the right problems, request the necessary actions and provide sources that support its conclusions.

03

Decisions within its authority

The agent proposes a decision. A separate check determines whether it is allowed to grant it. Requesting conditional approval does not mean that approval will be authorized.

A fourth condition is needed to claim a reduction in human work: total human hours must fall. A review that disappears from one team’s workload but reappears at a service provider has not been eliminated. An agent that sends every case to a person does not replace any reviews either. [3]

04The experiment

How can this be tested without using confidential company projects?

DGF-Bench uses 300 entirely fictional projects: 100 purchases, 100 integrations and 100 application developments. A program defines the facts for each project, draws its architecture and creates the documents needed for the reviews. It deliberately includes projects that meet the requirements and projects that do not. [4]

Each project contains about 26 Word documents, along with tables and an architecture diagram. These include a project charter, contracts, security test reports, operating procedures and backup, restore or load test results. Some documents are incomplete, outdated or inconsistent. A summary might say that a contract has been signed while the contract register still lists it as a draft. [4]

The agent does not create the project it reviews. It reads the project file, queries simulated company systems, requests missing information and produces the findings for each review. Those findings are passed to the next reviewer exactly as written, including any errors. No one corrects them along the way. [5]

The agent receives more than a pile of documents.

The protocol also supplies written rules and a structured summary of the project facts, as well as access to the information available through simulated systems. The separate file containing the answers used for scoring remains hidden. This information setting matters when interpreting the results. [4]

The same agent is run with three models: Gemini 3.8 Flash, GPT-5.6 Luna and DeepSeek v4.1 Flash. These are the model and endpoint names reported in the presentations for the tests on 22–23 September 2026. The settings are the same: temperature zero, at most 20 reasoning turns and 40 tool calls per review. [4]

The plan called for 1,700 reviews per model, or 900 complete project runs. One run was excluded after a technical failure at the model provider. The main results therefore cover 899 runs and 5,094 reviews. The presentations report $99.58 in model-call charges for the study, including failed attempts. That is not the cost of building or operating a production system in a company. [4]

05Understanding the scores

Who decides whether the agent passes? A program, not a human panel

When the program creates a project, it defines its facts: whether a contract is signed, whether the supplier has been checked or whether a required test has passed. It uses those facts and its programmed rules to determine the decision, the problems to report and the actions to request. It saves those answers for scoring. No human evaluator writes them. [5]

This information is stored in 99_hidden_ground_truth.json. The canonical_truth field describes the fictional project’s facts; reference_decisions contains the prepared answers for each review. The agent cannot read this file. Once the route is complete, the scoring program uses it to check the responses, also accounting for conditional approvals properly authorized during the run. [5]

For sources, the check goes beyond a plausible reference. The program inspects the tool-use logs to confirm that the agent actually opened the sources it cites. It also checks that quoted excerpts match the required passages word for word. A correct decision can therefore fail the test because its supporting quotation does not meet the protocol’s requirements. [5]

Authorization is checked at a different point. When an agent requests conditional approval, the tool checks whether its authority covers the review and project phase, whether every unresolved problem is included and whether those problems are eligible for conditional approval. The test checks that the required conditions are present; it does not establish that they will be effective in practice. [5]

Requesting a correction does not carry it out.

An agent can request a restore test. The benchmark does not then restore a backup on a real system. It evaluates the review and the decision. Automating the correction would also require executing the authorized action, recording its result and reviewing the project again. [5]

06The main results

100% correct decisions does not mean 100% successful reviews

Using Gemini, the agent makes the correct decision in all 1,694 evaluated reviews. Yet only 94.98% of those reviews meet all five scoring criteria, and only 76.92% of projects pass every review. Those numbers measure three different things. [6]

The first measures the decision itself. The second also requires a complete, properly sourced and authorized review. The third requires every review in a project to pass: a single failed review means the route fails. “77% of projects passed” therefore does not mean “77% of projects were approved.” [5][6]

With GPT-5.6 Luna and DeepSeek v4.1 Flash, strict review pass rates are 83.29% and 74.18%, respectively. Complete-route pass rates are 42.33% and 24.67%. The presentations separately report approximately 98% and 95% correct decisions for these two models. [6]

The main weakness: source citations

For Gemini, all 85 failed reviews fail solely because of a missing source citation or a quotation that does not meet the requirements. In Procurement, Luna and DeepSeek make the correct decision in 96% and 97% of reviews, but pass all criteria in only 33% and 43%. Their citations include a spreadsheet that the scoring program does not accept as a source. [6]

There are substantive errors as well. The presentations describe two: Luna approves a Legal review despite a missing data-processing agreement, while DeepSeek submits a placeholder GO when overlapping address ranges require NO_GO. No incorrect approval is reported for Gemini in the main tests. That describes the observed cases; it is not a guarantee for future projects. [6]

Why include a conventional program with no AI?

The fixed-rule comparison program scores 100% across all 1,700 reviews and 300 projects. That result matters: when the facts are already structured and the rules are explicit, conventional software may be enough. The question is what an agent adds when information is scattered, difficult to retrieve or contradictory. The benchmark does not establish that a language model is always necessary. [6]

What changes under a different citation-scoring rule?

The presentations report an alternative analysis that accepts the same values quoted in a different order. It would recover 69 additional reviews for Gemini, 38 for Luna and 52 for DeepSeek. Gemini would reach 99.06% review success and 94.98% complete-route success. This is a different scoring rule, not an improvement to the agent. The main results remain those shown in the charts above. [6]

07Two project files

Falcon and Meridian: what the agent actually does

Two examples from the execution logs make the scores easier to understand. They were selected after the evaluation to illustrate how it works, rather than to provide a separate measure of performance. [7]

Project FalconREVIEW PASSED

The operating instructions are still a draft

During the Tech Readiness review, the agent discovers that the runbook—the document explaining how to operate the application—has not been finalized.

FindingThe documents needed for the operations handover are incomplete.
Requested actionFinish the document and hand it over to the operations team.
DecisionGO WITH CONDITIONS, after the authorization check accepts the request.

The agent quotes the passage establishing the problem. The condition must be met before the application goes live; it remains open, with a named person responsible. The test does not claim that the document has already been completed. With each of the three models, all five Falcon reviews pass. [7]

Project MeridianCORRECT DECISION, FAILED REVIEW

The right findings, but an inexact quotation

The agent identifies two defects: a required API gateway is missing, and response time is 55 milliseconds rather than the required 20. It asks for the necessary corrections and makes the right decision: REWORK.

FindingsA missing gateway and an excessive response time.
AuthorizationTwo requests for conditional approval are rejected: the defects must be corrected.
ScoringOne quotation reverses the order of two facts. The review receives a strict score of zero.

The decision, findings and requested corrections are otherwise correct. The review scores 0.95 under the weighted method, but fails the strict method, which requires every criterion to pass. [7]

For this particular failure, the proposed fix is not necessarily a more capable model. It is a tool that attaches the exact source passage to each finding, rather than leaving the agent to reconstruct it. The study proposes this improvement; it does not present it as an already validated fix. [7]

08Reliability and authorization

The same project can pass one attempt and fail the next

After the main tests, 15 projects are reviewed three more times with each model, producing 135 additional project runs. The question is no longer just whether a project passed, but whether all of its reviews pass on every attempt. [8]

Across the 45 repeated runs per model, every review passes in 35 runs with Gemini, 19 with Luna and 11 with DeepSeek. Strict review pass rates are 96.08%, 82.75% and 73.33%. No incorrect approval or missed serious problem is reported in these 135 runs. The stated model-call cost of the repeat tests is $12.56. [8]

Authority cannot rest on the model’s own assurance

The presentations also report requests for conditional approval submitted to the authorization tool. They distinguish requests made, requests rejected and approvals eventually used in final decisions. These counts are not counts of unique projects. [8]

09Access to information

Better reasoning cannot recover information that was never provided

The study presents two variants of a project with identical text in all 26 Word documents. Yet the required decision is different. The difference appears in a separate record: the supplier has completed its background check in one case but not the other. [9]

This limitation applies to a person as well as an AI agent. Reasoning cannot supply a fact that the reviewer has never been given. Allowing access to the CSV record, or permitting a justified request for missing information, changes the task. [9]

The example helps explain the FDE’s role. Connecting an agent to the right records, controlling its access and defining what happens when information is missing are parts of the system—not details to address after choosing a model. In the study’s framework, access to information is a condition of a valid review. [3][9]

10From scores to employment

Automating 80% of cases does not remove 80% of the work

A pass rate does not measure hours saved. An agent may handle most straightforward cases while leaving specialists with the cases that take the longest. Human checks, error correction and platform operations add further work. [10]

Consider the example in the presentation. AI handles the easiest 80% of cases. Each remaining case takes twice the original average handling time, so the remaining 20% already accounts for 40% of the original workload. If the new working arrangement makes those cases take another 50% longer, they alone account for 60% of the original workload. Maintenance has not yet been added. [10]

Quality matters too. A mistake that goes undetected initially may create work later: someone has to find it, understand its consequences and correct it. A dangerous error left uncorrected does not become acceptable because the system is cheap to run. [10]

The equation used in the study
LA = λ [q hE + (1 − q) hR + hW] + BA

λ is the number of cases in the period; q is the share still handled by people; hE is human time per remaining case; hR is verification time for other cases; hW is average correction time per case; and BA is maintenance and support work during the period. The purpose is to add up actual hours, rather than infer them from the share of cases automated. [10]

Measuring a reduction therefore requires tracking comparable project types before and after automation: handling time, referrals to people, corrections and support. Consistent case identifiers help avoid counting the same task twice or overlooking work altogether. [10]

11Four ways to organize the work

The same automation can leave a need for 44 or 169 FTE

The study illustrates this with a starting workload of 140 full-time equivalents, or FTE. An FTE represents the amount of work associated with one full-time role; it is not necessarily a particular employee or position. The following four scenarios keep the same share of cases automated but change how the remaining work is organized. They are calculations based on assumptions, not staffing levels observed in a company. [11]

S0: AI also helps with complex cases. Specialists handle them in half the time, and the other cases receive limited checks. The result is 43.52 FTE, a reduction of about 69%. This is the most optimistic scenario and includes no maintenance time. [11]

S1: difficult cases take as long as before. Specialists handle them without AI assistance. Other cases receive brief checks, and each team assigns one person to keep the platform running. Required capacity rises to 85.32 FTE. S2 keeps those assumptions and adds half an hour of correction time per case, bringing the result to 89.07 FTE. [11]

S3: people recheck every decision. Complex cases take 50% longer; verification is more extensive; corrections take two hours per case; and two people per team run the platform. Required capacity reaches 168.92 FTE—about 21% more human work than before automation. [11]

The share of cases automated is not enough. What matters is how much time the organization still requires from people.

The study does not claim that any of these scenarios already describes a real deployment. They show why a benchmark result cannot be translated directly into a staffing reduction. [11]

12The longer study

How far could automation go—and on what timescale?

The longer study takes the hypothesis further: eventually, agents could perform every review, including complex cases and the final decision. The work of supervising them, maintaining rules and handling exceptions could then be automated too. Complete automation is not a result established by the benchmark. [12]

The required checks do not disappear in these scenarios. Human work falls as their execution is automated. Reaching zero also requires eliminating the work of the team operating the agents. As long as that work remains, it creates a minimum workload that cannot be ignored. [12]

Starting from 140 FTE, automating 80% of review work leaves 28 FTE of review work. Add five FTE to operate the platform, and the total is 33 FTE, not 28. To reach 28 FTE overall, about 84% of review work would have to be automated with a five-FTE support team, or about 91% with a fifteen-FTE team. [12]

A timeline built from explicit assumptions

The presentations also reproduce a timing calculation from the longer study. It starts with a cited May 2026 estimate: tasks equivalent to three hours of human work, completed successfully 80% of the time, with that task duration assumed to double every seven months. A typical review is assumed to represent 24 hours of human work. Three doublings would therefore take it to February 2028. These are inputs to the scenario, not measured durations for the benchmark’s reviews. [12]

Next, the error rate has to fall from 20% to roughly one failure in a thousand. The calculation then adds a year of testing with specialists and another year for organizational authorization. It varies the time needed for each tenfold reduction in the error rate: six, twelve or twenty-four months. [12]

Six predictions that make the hypothesis testable

H1Well-defined reviews are easier to automate.

Clear inputs, written rules and testable outputs should improve reliability, regardless of the model used.

H2A shared review record reduces duplicate work.

Passing findings and sources forward should reduce repeated work; agents should also produce the record at a lower cost.

H3The cases left to people take longer.

Their share of working hours should exceed their share of the case count.

H4Counting all remaining work improves the estimate.

Including complex cases, corrections and support should predict remaining hours better than a simple automation percentage.

H5Preparing decisions comes before authority to approve them.

Companies should let AI prepare decisions before delegating the corresponding decision rights.

H6Human-only tasks become fewer.

Tasks that currently require people should become automatable faster than new human-only tasks appear.

The hypothesis would be undermined if a review remained inherently human, if a human signature remained mandatory, if an “automated” service depended on hidden human work or if the support team could never be reduced. The longer study presents a conjecture to test, not a proven law. [12]

13A specific deadline

The author’s prediction: at least 80% fewer FTE by 2033

A PREDICTION SEPARATE FROM THE BENCHMARK RESULTS
−80%

by 20 September 2033

For the same DGF reviews, with a comparable workload, the same quality and the same turnaround time, relative to the baseline year ending 20 September 2026.

In the 140-FTE example, that means 28 FTE or fewer, including all human work. [13]

The author does not calculate this reduction from Gemini’s pass rate. It is a separate prediction with explicit failure conditions. The count must include people preparing and performing reviews, handling complex cases, checking results, correcting errors, maintaining and supervising agents, and providing supplier support. [13]

The prediction fails if more than 20% of the original human work remains, if quality deteriorates or if reviews take longer. Excluding the most difficult business units, overlooking new support functions or moving the deadline would also invalidate a claim of success. [13]

Without records of working hours, the result is unknown.

The English presentation states that no company had enrolled at the point described, even though the baseline year had ended. A new study could establish a new baseline year, but that would start another seven-year test—not move the 2033 deadline. [13]

What would this mean for the people doing the work today?

The division of work proposed in the study leaves people responsible for defining rules, making decisions that have not been delegated, providing required signatures, conducting spot checks and operating the system. People must also carry out the corrections requested by reviews until those actions are automated. Specialists could spend less time on individual files and more on designing, testing and maintaining decision rules. [13]

This does not guarantee that every job survives, or that every job disappears. A genuine reduction in required hours can eliminate positions; a company could instead keep the same team and handle more projects. Renaming people “AI supervisors” does not change the calculation. The unit being measured is work, regardless of job title. [13]

Could the same logic apply to insurance or banking?

The longer study considers activities with a similar structure: examine a file, apply rules, make a decision and explain it. Examples include underwriting, claims handling and credit analysis. DGF-Bench did not test these occupations. [13]

The presentations offer three external illustrations: at Allianz, agents process small claims while a person authorizes payment; at AIG, they prepare analysis while an underwriter decides; at Upstart, the presentations report that more than 90% of funded loans are approved without human review, while about a quarter of all applications are referred to a person. Funded loans and all applications are different denominators. These examples are reported as cited in the presentations, not as benchmark findings. [13]

Sector-specific rules and decisions that directly affect individuals require a different evaluation setting. Faster turnaround, a high automated-approval rate and fewer human working hours are not interchangeable measures.

14Conclusion

What the study establishes—and what remains to be tested

DGF-Bench shows agents carrying out reviews of fictional projects with defined access to documents, facts and rules. The best-performing model makes every correct decision in the evaluated sample, but not every review meets all five criteria. Exact source citations and consistency across repeated runs remain weaknesses. [6][8]

Type of conclusionWhat the presentations support
Measured in the experimentAgents completed 5,094 scored reviews across 899 project runs. Decision accuracy, strict review success and complete-route success are distinct measures.
Calculated under assumptionsDepending on the working arrangements, the 140-FTE example can result in roughly 44 or 169 FTE. Long-term automation levels and dates are illustrative.
Predicted by the authorAt least an 80% reduction in FTE for comparable work by 20 September 2033; in the longer term, automation of every review and its supervision.
Not established by these testsSuperiority to human specialists, real-company hours saved, the practical effectiveness of requested corrections or the order in which sectors will adopt AI.

The presentations describe the release of project files, agent responses, tool-use logs, scoring code and cost records. They explain that the reported results can be recalculated from those materials without making new paid calls to the models. [14]

The review remains mandatory. The questions are who performs it, what information they can access, which decisions they may make—and how much work they leave for everyone else.
